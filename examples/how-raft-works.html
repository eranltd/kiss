Servers elect one leader, and a change counts once most of them have it.

The leader of your five-server store crashes, and within about one election timeout (the paper suggests 150–300 ms) a follower takes over. Your next SET x=5 is safe once three of the five have written it down.

Marked: the majority, because three of five decides both who leads and which lines are final. The drawings below zoom into each part.

In plain words. Think of a committee where every member keeps the same notebook. Only the chair writes new lines; the others copy them. A line counts once most members have copied it. If the chair goes quiet, the members vote in a new one.

  1. A follower that stops hearing from the leader starts an election.

    In plain words. The chair keeps sending a short "still here" note. A member that hears nothing before its egg timer runs out runs for chair.

    Under the hood. A heartbeat is an AppendEntries RPC with no entries. A follower that gets none within its election timeout adds 1 to its currentTerm, becomes a candidate, votes for itself and sends RequestVote to every other server. Each server picks its timeout at random, say from 150–300 ms, so one usually fires alone.

    committee = clustermember = serverchair = leader"still here" note = heartbeategg timer = election timeoutrunning for chair = candidate

  2. A candidate needs a majority, and each server votes once per term.

    In plain words. Each election gets a new term number. A member gives one ballot per term, to the first candidate who asks, unless that candidate's notebook is behind its own. Three ballots of five win.

    Under the hood. RequestVote carries the candidate's term, lastLogIndex and lastLogTerm. A server grants it if its votedFor is empty and the candidate's last entry has a later term, or the same term and an index at least as high. A split vote times out and retries in a higher term.

    ballot = RequestVoteterm number = termnotebook = log

    Any two majorities share a server, so a term never has two leaders.

  3. The leader writes your command as a new line and sends it to every follower.

    In plain words. The chair writes SET x=5 as line 7 and sends it in a note. The note also says what line 6 should look like. A member whose line 6 matches copies line 7; otherwise it says no, and the chair resends from an earlier line.

    Under the hood. Each entry stores its command and its term. A follower rejects an AppendEntries whose prevLogIndex and prevLogTerm it lacks; the leader lowers its nextIndex for that follower and retries, and the follower drops conflicting entries. So logs that agree on one entry agree on everything before it.

    AppendEntries {
  term: 3, leaderId: "s1",
  prevLogIndex: 6, prevLogTerm: 3,
  entries: [
    { term: 3, command: "SET x=5" }
  ],
  leaderCommit: 6
}
    line = log entrynote = AppendEntries

  4. A line is final once a majority has it, and then every server applies it.

    In plain words. Once three of five notebooks hold line 7, no later chair can lose it. The chair carries it out and answers you OK. Its next notes tell the others to carry it out too.

    Under the hood. The leader moves commitIndex to 7, the highest entry from its own term that a majority holds (it tracks a matchIndex per follower). It applies the entry to its state machine, then replies. Later AppendEntries carry leaderCommit, and followers apply up to it in log order.

    final line = committed entrycarry out = apply to state machine

    A write is done when three of five have it, not all five.

An old leader does not know it was replaced.

Cut off from the majority, it still thinks it leads, so a read it serves can be stale. Before answering a read, a Raft leader must hear back from a majority in one heartbeat round.

Push further

Why do clusters have 3 or 5 servers, not 4?

Four servers need three for a majority, so they survive one failure, the same as three. Five servers survive two.

What happens when the network splits into 2 and 3?

The three elect a leader in a higher term and keep writing; the two commit nothing. After the split heals, the old leader sees the higher term, steps down and loses its uncommitted lines.

Can a new leader commit an old leader's line by counting copies?

No. A leader counts copies only for lines from its own term; older lines are committed along with a later line from its term. The paper's Figure 8 shows why.
