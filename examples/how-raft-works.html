An elected leader copies each write; a majority makes it final.

You send SET x=5 to three servers. One round trip later, the leader says OK: two of the three hold a copy.

Marked: the majority, because a write is final once most servers hold it. The drawings below zoom into each part.

In plain words. Raft runs a cluster like a club with an elected secretary. The secretary writes each decision in the minutes and mails every new line to members. A line is final once most members have copied it. If the secretary goes quiet, a member calls an election for the next term.

  1. Every server is a follower, a candidate or the leader.

    In plain words. Members listen. A member who stops hearing from the secretary runs for the job. Anyone who learns of a newer term goes back to listening.

    Under the hood. Time is cut into numbered terms (currentTerm), and each term has at most one leader. Every message carries the sender's term: a server that sees a higher term adopts it and becomes a follower, and a message with an older term is rejected. Every server starts as a follower.

  2. A follower that hears nothing runs for leader.

    In plain words. Each member has an alarm clock with a random setting. The first to ring asks the others for votes. Two votes out of three make it the secretary.

    Under the hood. The election timeout is random, 150–300 ms in the paper (1). S2 raises its term to 2, votes for itself and sends RequestVote (2); each server grants one vote per term, so S3's yes makes a majority (3), and S2 sends empty AppendEntries as heartbeats (4). With fixed timeouts, split votes made the paper's elections take over 10 s.

  3. A write is final once two of three servers store it.

    In plain words. The secretary writes the line, mails it out, and waits for one member to confirm. Then it applies the line and tells you OK. The third server gets the same letter, but nobody waits for it.

    Under the hood. The leader appends the entry to its log[] and sends AppendEntries to every follower in parallel (2). Each message names the entry just before the new ones (prevLogIndex, prevLogTerm); a follower that lacks it says no, and the leader backs up one entry and retries. After a majority stores it (3), the leader moves commitIndex forward, applies the entry and replies (4); with five servers, three copies are a majority, so two servers may fail.

  4. Only a server with every committed entry can win.

    In plain words. A member votes only for candidates with minutes at least as complete as its own. So a member that missed lines cannot become the secretary.

    Under the hood. RequestVote carries the candidate's last log index and term (lastLogIndex, lastLogTerm) (1). The voter refuses if its own log is more up to date: a later last term wins, then a longer log (2). A committed entry sits on a majority and a winner needs a majority of votes, so some voter always holds it, and S1 wins next (3).

A read can return old data, even from the leader.

A leader cut off by a partition can serve stale values without knowing it. Before a read, it confirms with a majority, once its blank no-op entry has committed.

What happens when the network splits two and one?

The two-server side elects a new leader in a higher term. The lone old leader commits nothing and steps down once it sees that term.

Can a client's retry run a write twice?

Yes, if the leader commits and crashes before it replies. The paper tags each command with a serial number, so repeats are skipped.

Why does the commit loop check the entry's term?

A leader counts copies only for its own term's entries; older ones commit beneath them. In the paper's Figure 8, an old entry on a majority still gets overwritten.

Simplified on purpose. Three servers instead of the typical five; no membership changes, snapshots (InstallSnapshot) or faster log back-off.
