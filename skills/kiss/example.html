Your server hands it to theirs, and theirs checks the seals.

You press Send on a note from alice@example.com to bob@example.net. Moments later it sits in Bob's inbox, after two hand-offs and three checks.

  1. Your appMUA
  2. Your post office:587
  3. Address lookupDNS MX
  4. Bob's post office:25
  5. Seal checkSPF · DKIM · DMARC
  6. Bob's drawerIMAP

Marked: the check at Bob's door, where a letter is let in, sent to Junk or turned away. The drawings below zoom into each part.

In plain words. Think of two post offices. Your app drops the letter at yours, which looks up Bob's and hands it over. Bob's checks the postmark and the seal, files it in Bob's drawer, and Bob's app reads it there.

  1. Your app posts the letter at your own post office, with a key.

    In plain words. Your app never talks to Bob's server. It drops the letter in your own post office's box, and the box opens only with your login.

    Under the hood. The app (the MUA) connects to your server's submission port, 587, and logs in with SMTP AUTH. By default, the submission server must refuse MAIL from a session that has not logged in.

    your app = MUAyour post office = MSApost box = port 587key = SMTP AUTH

  2. Your post office looks up Bob's post office in DNS.

    In plain words. Your server reads the part after the @ and asks DNS who takes mail for example.net. The answer is a short list, best first.

    Under the hood. Each MX record names a host and a preference. Lower numbers go first, so mx2 gets the letter only when mx1 does not answer. With no MX record at all, the domain itself is the target (an implicit MX).

    $ dig +short MX example.net
10 mx1.example.net.   # example
20 mx2.example.net.
    card file = DNScard row = MX record

  3. After one receipt, the letter is Bob's server's job.

    In plain words. The two servers talk in short commands on port 25. The envelope goes first, then the letter, then a receipt comes back.

    Under the hood. MAIL FROM and RCPT TO are the envelope. The From: line Bob sees is inside the letter, sent after DATA. A 4xx reply means try later: wait at least 30 minutes between tries, and keep trying for 4–5 days. A 5xx reply means no, and Alice gets a bounce.

    C: MAIL FROM:<alice@example.com>
S: 250 OK
C: RCPT TO:<bob@example.net>
S: 250 OK
C: DATA
S: 354 Start mail input
C: From: alice@example.com … "."
S: 250 OK
    front door = port 25 (SMTP)envelope = MAIL FROM + RCPT TOreceipt = 250 reply

  4. Bob's server trusts the letter only if the postmark or the seal matches the sender.

    In plain words. The postmark shows which post office posted it. The wax seal shows nobody changed it. The rule book, written by example.com, says what to do with a fake.

    Under the hood. SPF reads a v=spf1 TXT record listing the servers allowed to send for the envelope's domain. DKIM checks the DKIM-Signature header against a public key at <selector>._domainkey.example.com; a changed body breaks it. DMARC (_dmarc.example.com: p=none, quarantine or reject) passes when SPF or DKIM passes and its domain matches the From: domain.

    postmark = SPFwax seal = DKIM signaturerule book = DMARC policybin = reject

    SPF and DKIM prove some domain. DMARC asks if it is the one in From.

  5. A clerk files it in a drawer, and Bob's app reads it there.

    In plain words. A spam filter reads the letter and picks a drawer: Inbox or Junk. Bob's app opens the drawer with IMAP. The letter stays in the cabinet, so Bob's laptop sees it too.

    Under the hood. IMAP listens on port 993 (TLS) or 143. INBOX is the main mailbox, and flags such as \Seen live on the server. With IDLE, the server tells the app the moment a letter lands (* 4 EXISTS).

    clerk = spam filtercabinet = mailbox storedrawer = IMAP mailbox

The From line Bob sees is not the address SPF checks.

SPF checks the envelope (MAIL FROM), so a spammer can pass it with their own domain while the letter says From: you@bank.example. Only DMARC ties the checks to the visible From:.

Push further

Why does forwarding break SPF but not DKIM?

The forwarder posts it again from its own server, which the SPF record of example.com does not list. The DKIM seal rides inside the letter and survives unless someone edits the signed parts.

Why did my mail arrive an hour late?

Bob's server can answer 4xx, which means try later. Your server then waits at least 30 minutes before the next try.

How do I see which checks passed?

Open the raw message and find the Authentication-Results header. Bob's server writes the spf=, dkim= and dmarc= results there.

Simplified on purpose. One hop each way; real mail can pass more relays, and TLS between servers is left out.

Sources: RFCs 5321 (SMTP), 6409 (submission), 7208 (SPF), 6376 (DKIM), 7489 (DMARC), 9051 (IMAP) and 8601. Not verified: how spam filters pick a drawer; no RFC defines it.
